# Getting Your First 10 Customers

The strategy of early-stage sales is covered elsewhere in this library — do things that don't
scale, founder-led sales, charge from day one (`../stages/05_mvp_build.md`, `../frameworks/pmf.md`).
This playbook is the **tactics**: how you actually find your first ten paying customers and start the
conversation. It is distinct from the *research* outreach in `run_outreach.md` and `cold_email.md` —
those get you interviews with the seven stakeholder categories; this gets you **revenue** from a
desperate buyer once you have something to sell (Stage 05 → 06).

The one frame to carry through all of it: for the first ten customers, **you, the founder, doing
unscalable things, beat any tool.** Showing up signals something automation can't fake — that you
cared enough about this problem to spend your own time on it. That is the one advantage you have over
every established company in your space. Lean into it.

## Step 0 — Where does your buyer actually spend their time?

Before you touch a tool or pick a channel, spend an hour answering this concretely. Most founders
default to cold email and LinkedIn because it's easy, feels like work, and has endless tooling. For
some buyers — a sales leader, a developer — that's right: they live on a laptop and in their inbox.
But a school-district administrator, a property manager, an insurance agent, a truck dispatcher, a
farm GM does **not** sit on a laptop all day and does not treat email as their primary surface. Blast
them with cold email and you'll wrongly conclude "outbound doesn't work for us."

Answer these about your buyer — concretely, not in the abstract:

- What does their average working day look like?
- How often do they check email — and is it their main work surface, or a backwater?
- Do they go to conferences or trade shows? *Which ones?*
- Are they active on any community — Reddit, LinkedIn, a trade forum, a Facebook group, a Discord?
- Do they pick up the phone?
- Do they find new tools by asking peers for recommendations?
- Are there industry newsletters they actually read?

**If you can't answer most of these concretely, that is the signal — you haven't spent enough time
with real customers yet.** Fix that first (back to `../stages/02_customer_discovery.md`); the channel
falls out of the answer. The most common expensive mistake here is months refining email subject
lines for a buyer who was never in their inbox, when three days walking a trade-show floor would have
closed more.

## The customer-count ladder

Where customers come from changes as you go. Don't reach for the next rung before you've worked the
current one.

### Customers 1–3 — your warm network, almost without exception

The first few customers come from people who already have reason to trust *you*: friends in the
industry, former colleagues, classmates, or someone one introduction away. This isn't a sales trick.
Early buyers are betting on the founder as much as the product — a new product from a new company
needs a person they trust behind it, and your first- and second-degree network are the people most
willing to take that bet.

Work these sources in order:

1. **Personal network** — former colleagues, classmates, friends in the industry. Highest trust;
   start here.
2. **Second-degree connections** — look at who your network knows and ask for warm intros. (Founders
   routinely close a large share of their first customers this way.)
3. **Network-search tools** — natural-language search across your extended network to surface
   relevant people you'd never find scrolling manually.

When you ask for an intro, make it trivial to act on: say exactly **who** you want to meet, **why
they specifically** would care, and **what to write** in the forwarded note. The easier you make it,
the more often it actually happens.

> **Don't set up prospecting tools yet.** Apollo/Clay/sequencers start to matter at ~10–20 customers.
> Founders routinely burn weeks configuring outreach tooling while sitting on dozens of un-messaged
> second-degree connections. Working the network is the lowest-hanging fruit; do it first.

### Customers 4–10 — things that don't scale

This rung is manual, tedious, and the point. A handful of tactics that work far better than their
discomfort suggests:

- **Show up in person — get in the room.** Not a Zoom; the same physical room as the buyer. It's
  slower, awkward, sometimes costs travel, and you can get rejected to your face. It also converts
  better than anything else. Founders have flown to one buyer week after week through reschedules and
  eventually closed them; persistence in person is itself the signal.
- **Small, specific conferences > large ones.** One real hallway conversation can out-convert a
  hundred cold emails to the same person. The mini-playbook founders reuse: set up a Calendly with
  back-to-back 15-minute slots across every day of the event; email the attendee list a couple of
  times beforehand to fill slots; email again *during* the event to catch non-openers; stack meetings
  all day.
- **Micro-events.** A founder dinner or happy hour for 6–10 people in your exact ICP (≈$50–100/head)
  converts far better than a big event. Once someone has had dinner with you, they rarely ignore your
  follow-up — and often go out of their way to help.
- **Go where the pain is already public.** For consumer/SMB products there's usually a place your
  customers already complain about the problem — Reddit, Facebook groups, Discord, YouTube comments;
  for B2B niches, trade forums or association boards. Find old threads where people describe your exact
  problem and DM commenters one by one. (Expect to get torn up in the comments while lurkers quietly
  sign up; one founder's first ten all came from DMing old complaint threads. Reddit threads also
  persist in Google for years, so the work compounds.)

### Customers 10–50 — now the playbook shifts to tools

By here you have a refined pitch, case studies, and a real grasp of what resonates — so higher-volume
outreach finally pays. Find ICP-matching companies, the right person at each, their contact info, and
reach out at volume:

- **Apollo** — lead database + email finding + a basic sequencer. Generous free tier; for most
  founders at this stage, enough on its own. The usual starting point.
- **Clay** — research/enrichment workflows on top of your list; matters when you qualify on something
  specific (what software they use, who's hiring, who posted about a topic recently).
- **LinkedIn premium** — richest fresh professional data. Often: connection request with no message,
  then a short DM once they accept.

Only here does scaling make sense, because only here do you have a message worth scaling.

## Outreach framing — the foot in the door

For early outreach, the highest-converting framing is often **not** a sales pitch. Genuine versions of
"can I learn from you" open doors a pitch can't:

- **Ask for advice / mentorship.** Short, real messages to people in your space asking if they'd
  advise you. Most are flattered; some accept; a few become customers.
- **Run user research as the outreach.** Max out connection requests around one specific hypothesis
  per week; convert a slice to calls. By the time you have a product, you have a pipeline of people
  who already told you what they need.
- **Pay for feedback.** In high-contract-value markets, offering a portion of someone's hourly rate
  for genuine product feedback can convert a meaningful share — and the loaded CAC still pencils out
  because the conversion is so high (sanity-check it with `../frameworks/unit_economics.md`).

> **The honesty line:** only use these framings if you *actually* want to learn. Disguising a sales
> call as a mentorship ask, with no real intent to listen, poisons the relationship and the data —
> the same Mom Test failure (`../frameworks/the_mom_test.md`) that corrupts discovery.

### Give something before you ask

The highest-converting cold outreach often delivers value *before* asking for anything: a quick
security scan of the prospect's public site, two specific suggestions after walking through their
onboarding, a short audit note specific to their product. ~20 minutes of work before asking for 30 of
their time is a fair trade — and deliberately unscalable, which is the point at this stage.

## Writing the message

Exact copy matters less than founders think; a few things matter a lot:

- **Under ~75 words.** Long emails get ignored and read as LLM-written.
- **One clear call to action.** State exactly what you want — a reply, a call, a 15-minute demo.
  Without it, they assume the worst (that you'll eat their time) and pass.
- **The read-aloud test.** Read the email to a friend. Anything you wouldn't actually *say* to a real
  person, rewrite. A one-minute check that strips the AI-sounding lines that ruin cold emails.
- **Follow up 3–4 times over a couple of weeks.** Most positive replies come on a follow-up, not the
  first touch.

## Why this phase works (and when to stop)

The messy 4-to-10 stretch works **because you personally are doing it.** A founder who shows up at an
office, DMs a Reddit commenter, or sends a researched note only someone who studied the problem could
write, signals care no tool can fake. That founder signal is your edge over incumbents — it's the one
thing they can't buy. You don't have to be a great salesperson; you have to be willing to do the
unscalable things most people won't.

Stop hand-cranking when the same pitch is closing the same ICP repeatably (~10–20 paying in the narrow
*who*) — that's the cue the message is worth scaling, and the moment the 10–50 tools earn their place.
